How DCF valuation works — a plain-English guide
  1. Estimate future cash flows — Forecast what free cash flow the business will generate each year over the projection period. The tool seeds this from Yahoo Finance data and your growth rate input.
  2. Discount them to today — A dollar earned in 5 years is worth less than a dollar today. We divide each year's cash flow by (1 + discount rate)year to get its present value.
  3. Add a terminal value — Beyond the forecast period, the business keeps operating. Terminal value captures all future cash flows as a perpetuity growing at the terminal growth rate. This often drives 60–80% of the total value — treat it with care.
  4. Adjust for debt and cash — Subtract net debt (total debt minus cash) from firm value to get equity value. A cash-rich company is worth more per share.
  5. Divide by shares outstanding — Equity value ÷ shares = intrinsic value per share. Compare to current price to find margin of safety and expected return.
How the Dividend Discount Model works — a plain-English guide
  1. Why not DCF? — For banks and insurers, "free cash flow" is meaningless: deposits and reserves are raw materials, not owner earnings, and there is no clean capital structure to derive WACC from. The DDM values the stock from what it actually pays shareholders — dividends.
  2. Project the dividend — Start from the current annual dividend per share and grow it each year by your dividend growth rate over the projection period.
  3. Discount to today — Each future dividend is divided by (1 + cost of equity)year. Cost of equity comes from CAPM (risk-free rate + beta × equity risk premium) — no debt component.
  4. Add a terminal value — Beyond the forecast, dividends are valued as a perpetuity growing at the terminal growth rate (Gordon Growth Model), then discounted back.
  5. Sum to intrinsic value per share — Because dividends are already per share, the total is the intrinsic value per share directly — no net-debt or share-count step. Compare to current price for margin of safety and expected return.

About this tool

Enter any stock ticker to estimate its intrinsic value using Discounted Cash Flow analysis. The tool fetches live data from Yahoo Finance, calculates WACC from first principles (cost of equity via CAPM, cost of debt from income statement), and runs three scenarios — Bear, Base, and Bull — so you can see the full range of possible outcomes.

Results show intrinsic value per share, margin of safety versus today's price, and expected annualised return if the stock converges to fair value over 1, 3, or 5 years. Financial sector companies (banks, insurance) automatically trigger a reliability warning with normalised FCF input and alternative multiples. Designed for individual stocks with meaningful positive free cash flow.

Frequently asked questions

What is DCF analysis?

Discounted Cash Flow analysis estimates what a business is worth today based on the cash it is expected to generate in the future. Future cash flows are "discounted" back to present value because money received later is worth less than money received now. The result is an intrinsic value — what the stock should theoretically be worth if the market were perfectly rational.

What discount rate (WACC) should I use?

This tool calculates WACC automatically from Yahoo Finance data using the CAPM formula for cost of equity (Risk-free rate + Beta × Equity risk premium) and interest expense for cost of debt. As a rough guide: stable large-caps sit around 7–9%, growth companies 10–12%, and higher-risk businesses 12–15%. You can override the calculated value directly in the input fields.

Why does terminal value matter so much?

Terminal value (the value beyond the forecast period) often represents 60–80% of the total DCF result. This makes the terminal growth rate assumption extremely important — a 0.5% change in terminal growth can shift intrinsic value by 10–20%. When the tool flags "terminal value >70%", treat the result with extra caution and test different terminal growth assumptions.

Why does the tool show a warning for insurance and financial companies?

Insurance and bank free cash flows include large investment portfolio movements and reserve changes that aren't true "owner earnings." Raw DCF for these companies routinely overstates value significantly. The tool prompts you to enter a normalised FCF — a conservative estimate of sustainable cash earnings — and also shows P/E, P/B, and ROE as more reliable valuation cross-checks for this sector.
